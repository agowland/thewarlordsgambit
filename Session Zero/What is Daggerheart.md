# Intro to Daggerheart

## What is Daggerheart?

- Daggerheart is a story-driven heroic fantasy game that’s got some of the crunch of D&D and some of the open-ended collaborative storytelling of systems like Powered By The Apocalypse.
- Runs on a unique “Duality Dice” mechanic driven by 2 different coloured D12s that result in a “Roll with Hope” or a “Roll with Fear”. These rolls create cinematic, mixed-success outcomes rather than “good” or ”bad” ones.
- “The Golden Rule” of Daggerheart is that the core book is *not* meant to be a “be-all, end-all”, only a jumping off point. In other words, “The rules should never get in the way of the story you want to tell, the characters you want to play, or the adventures you want to have.” Every rule is open to being modified modified or ignored – with the rest of the table’s consent.
- “The game takes a fiction-first approach, encouraging players and GMs to act in good faith with one another and focus on the story they’re telling rather than the complexity of the mechanics.” (DCR, 4)

## Core Mechanics

- Character development (in a way that prioritizes story over levels or on-paper abilities) is key to Daggerheart, and a fair amount of time in Session Zero is spent figuring out who characters are as people and how they may be connected. The GM uses this information to help ensure that the story revolves around the characters, rather than fitting the characters into a prepared story.
- **The Spotlight** is one way that the story is made more immersive and real. The GM gives each character their moment in the spotlight, moving the story forward through ‘snapshots’ and revealing more of each situation by going around the table one-by-one.
	- The GM is **always** the heroes’ biggest fan, but each player should also be a fan of the other players, and make sure to think of how each player’s story can move forward in any given situation, not just their own.
- Combat has **no initiative or rounds** and, instead, progresses organically through narrative. Just like in non-combat scenes, players describe what their characters are doing, following their character’s story and what makes sense for it, rather than a set of rules. However, any time the GM thinks a character is trying to do something that might be a) contested by another character (PC or NPC) or b) difficult for an ordinary person under ordinary circumstances, they’ll likely ask for a roll.
- **Duality Dice**! Non-combat rolls are done using two d12s, each with a different colour – one colour represents Hope, and the other colour represents Fear. After rolling, the sum of the two dice determines whether it’s a success or a failure (depending on what difficulty the GM has set), and whichever die shows the highest number (be it the Hope die or the Fear die) determines whether the player gets an advantage or the GM does. That advantage doesn’t need to be used in that specific instant though – when collected over time, Hope points can unlock powerful abilities for players, and Fear points unlock powerful abilities for the GM and adversaries.
-  **Advantage and Disadvantage** work a little bit differently than in other games. When you roll with Advantage, you add a 1d6 to your roll. When you roll with Disadvantage, you roll a 1d6 in addition to your normal dice, but you subtract its total from your roll. 
- In **combat**, players pass the spotlight back and forth amongst themselves, describing what their characters do until someone fails a roll against an adversary or rolls with Fear. Or if the GM decides to spend a Fear point to interrupt the proceedings. Then, the adversaries have the spotlight…dundundun.
- **Maps and movement** are determined by a scale of ‘closeness’. Using a squared map works just the same as in D&D, with each square representing 5’. But players and the GM can also use a more ‘squishy’ scale to determine ranges of movement and attack: Melee, Very Close, Close, Far, Very Far and Out of Range. It’s up to the table how precise they want to be.
